# The q = 24 restriction cannot extend to the archived residual

## Result, scope, and nonredundancy

For the archived residual B of length 37, in the one-dimensional left-projection-kernel branch, **q = 24 is impossible**. Combined with the earlier q >= 16, q divisible by four, and November 9 q = 28 obstruction, only q = 16 and q = 20 remain for this particular branch. Neither remaining value is excluded here.

This is not a target-code construction, a complete branch exclusion, a result about other residuals or the zero-kernel branch, or a proof of global binary [64,11,27] nonexistence. No novelty or external peer-review claim is made. The proof uses classical binary-code duality and character orthogonality, with exact finite checks for the archived residual.

The previously published q24 formal point (a,b,c,d,e,f,g) = (6,14,4,6,13,0,18), with seven counts (28,24,76,32,18,44,18), still passes all 11 domain bounds, all 1,064 integral nonnegative split-transform coefficients, and the November 8 incidence cut with slack 2. Our checker reruns these exact tests. The present proof excludes all actual realizations of q24, including every labelling of this point. Thus it is a nonredundant strengthening of those aggregate conditions, not a retraction of their formal feasibility.

The new ingredient beyond the first two moments is a four-message block identity from the classified q24 restriction. The first two moments alone do not exclude zero weight on the two zero-on-S columns. In particular, replacing the variable repeated-column moment by a fixed distinct-column moment would be invalid.

## Classification of the restriction, with proof for every orientation

There are 24 nonzero messages of left weight 12 and seven of weight 16. Write H for the seven heavy messages.

The first moment is 24*12 + 7*16 = 400. Each nonzero restricted column contributes 16 to this sum. Hence A has 25 columns nonzero on S and two columns zero on S.

For each nonzero restricted column label v in F2^5, let m_v be its multiplicity. Character inversion of the weight function gives

m_v = -(1/16) sum_s wt(sA) (-1)^(s dot v)
    = (3 - sum_(s in H) (-1)^(s dot v))/4
    = (k_v - 2)/2,

where k_v counts the messages of H on which v evaluates to one. Since 0 <= k_v <= 7 and m_v is a nonnegative integer, k_v belongs to {2,4,6} and m_v belongs to {0,1,2}. A triple column is therefore impossible, even though the bare repeated-pair count of three would allow one.

H spans F2^5: otherwise some nonzero v annihilates H and gives m_v = -1. The seven distinct nonzero columns H therefore generate an even binary [7,5] evaluation code E. It is even because every k_v is even. Its dual E-perp has dimension two and contains the all-ones vector. Since the seven columns are nonzero and pairwise distinct, E-perp has no weight-one or weight-two word. If a is either of its other nonzero words, both a and its complement have weight at least three, so their weights are three and four.

Consequently H splits into a triple with sum zero and a complementary quadruple with sum zero. The triple is P minus {0} for a two-dimensional message subspace P. The four distinct nonzero messages of the quadruple span a three-dimensional subspace V. Since all H span five dimensions, P and V are complementary. A choice of bases transports H to

H = {1,2,3,4,8,16,28},

where P = {0,1,2,3} and the quadruple is {4,8,16,28}. This proves classification for arbitrary actual restrictions, not just the canonical control.

The multiplicity formula now gives nine absent nonzero labels, 19 single labels, and three doubled labels. Explicitly the doubled labels are {29,30,31}. Among the 25 active coordinate positions, exactly one column vanishes on P, namely the single column v0 = 28. The other 24 positions restrict nontrivially to P. This property transports with the chosen basis; no particular orientation of P is assumed for the target residual.

Equivalently, on the complement V the four heavy messages form an affine plane not containing zero. Its evaluation has weight four on exactly one nonzero functional and weight two on the other six. This proves directly the unique-active-column property used next.

## The correct second moment includes all repeated-column cross terms

Fix a quotient t. Let z(t) in {0,1,2} be its weight on the two coordinate positions vanishing on S. For each nonzero v, define the signed multiplicity

a_v(t) = sum_(columns j restricting to v) (-1)^(value of column j at t).

Then

w_t(s) = wt((t XOR (s << 4))A)
       = 25/2 + z(t) - (1/2) sum_(v nonzero) a_v(t) (-1)^(s dot v).

Character orthogonality yields the exact centered energy

sum_s (w_t(s) - 25/2 - z(t))^2 = 8 sum_v a_v(t)^2.

Each of the 19 singles contributes one to the last sum. Each double contributes either zero or four according to whether its signs disagree or agree. Hence

energy(t) = 152 + 32 c(t),  where c(t) is in {0,1,2,3}.

The possible energies are 152,184,216,248. In the equivalent cross-term expression this is 200 + 16 times the sum of the three pair-sign products. Neither 200 nor 248 can be imposed as a constant on all cosets.

## New four-message block identity

Partition S into its eight cosets of the classified two-dimensional P. Each of the 24 active positions nonzero on P contributes two ones to each four-message block. The unique active column v0 vanishing on P contributes either zero or four ones. Let b_t(B) be its bit on the block B. The two S-zero positions contribute 4z(t). Thus

sum_(s in B) w_t(s) = 48 + 4z(t) + 4b_t(B).

Because v0 is a nonzero functional on S/P, exactly four blocks have b_t(B)=0 and four have b_t(B)=1, regardless of its affine offset at t. This is the additional information that the aggregate first and second moments do not retain.

## Actual residual windows force z(t) to be nonzero on ten quotient labels

For each t in T = {2,3,5,6,8,9,10,11,12,14}, direct calculation of the residual bounds for u and u+h and the mod-four condition gives a nonzero affine hyperplane n_t dot s = c_t. Its 16 messages have forced left weight 14; all other 16 allow exactly {10,14,18}.

The certificate (t,n_t,c_t) is

(2,22,0), (3,26,0), (5,8,0), (6,13,1), (8,10,1),
(9,19,1), (10,14,0), (11,28,0), (12,29,0), (14,23,0).

The checker recomputes all windows from B, rather than treating this table as an unchecked premise.

Fix one t and suppose z(t)=0. There are two exhaustive cases.

1. P lies in the direction of the forced-14 hyperplane. Four P-blocks consist entirely of weight-14 messages. Each would sum to 56, whereas the block formula gives either 48 or 52. This is impossible.

2. P is transverse to that hyperplane. Every block contains two forced 14s and two free weights x,y in {10,14,18}. If b_t(B)=0, the block equation is 28+x+y=48, forcing x=y=10. If b_t(B)=1, it is 28+x+y=52, forcing {x,y}={10,14}.

There are four blocks of each kind, so the whole coset contains twelve weights 10 and twenty weights 14. Since z=0, its mean is 25/2. Its centered energy is

12*(10 - 25/2)^2 + 20*(14 - 25/2)^2
= 12*(25/4) + 20*(9/4) = 120.

But every actual q24 extension has energy at least 152. Contradiction.

Therefore z(t) is nonzero for every t in T. The proof did not require choosing or enumerating a particular target orientation. As a redundant finite check, the script covers all 155 two-dimensional P, all seven nonzero v0 annihilating each P, both v0 offsets, and all ten target hyperplanes: 21,700 cases, comprising 4,900 parallel and 16,800 transverse cases.

## Two zero-on-S columns cannot have this zero set

Those two coordinate positions annihilate S and h. They define a linear map

Z : F2^4 -> F2^2,

whose output weight is z(t). Its kernel has dimension at least two, so it contains three distinct nonzero elements a,b,a XOR b.

We have just excluded every member of T from that kernel. The remaining five nonzero labels are

C = {1,4,7,13,15}.

The ten distinct pairwise XORs of C are exactly T. In particular, C contains no triple a,b,a XOR b. The required kernel cannot exist. This proves the q24 exclusion.

The finite enumeration of all 136 unordered two-column maps (including zero and repeated columns) is a redundant terminal check, not a substitute for this linear-algebra argument.
